# Kidoboard

A screen to do things with, not to watch. Words, numbers, shapes and a second language for children from two to five, played with a grown-up, on a keyboard, a mouse or a touchscreen.

One file, no build, no account, no tracking: open `index.html`. English and French. Real photos, real recordings.

## What is in it

Tell the app roughly how old the child is (2, 3, 4 or 5). That sets where each subject starts and how big the keys are. From there the app follows the child, not the age.

**Learn.** One question at a time, two or three pictures. Answer by touch, with the mouse, or with any key on that side of the keyboard.

1. **Words.** "Where is the cow?" Animals first, colours later.
2. **A second language.** The same pictures, asked in the other language (English if the app is in French, French if it is in English).
3. **Numbers.** One, then two, then three; which has more; four and five; then the written numbers.
4. **Shapes.** Circle, square, triangle, rectangle, in plain and odd versions; big and small; what comes next in a pattern.

**The keyboard.** One press, one thing.

1. **Press.** Any press shows an animal, says its name, plays its call.
2. **Count.** Every press adds one animal, counted aloud. Later the child must stop at the right number.
3. **Find.** "Where does the cow live?" Its key lights up, less and less.
4. **Race.** Hop along the numbers 1 to 10, one press per hop.

**The mouse.** Move, then Click, then Drag. After that, every Learn game can be answered with it.

**For the grown-up.** A page called "How to help, and why" explains each game: what to say next to the child, and the finding behind it. Also: a sitting that ends by itself (5, 10 or 15 minutes), key sizes (six big keys, twelve, or every key), your own voice instead of the computer's, printable stickers for the real keys. Progress stays in the browser.

On a phone or tablet everything is played with a finger. iPhone and iPad have no full screen for web pages: use Share, then Add to Home Screen, and open Kidoboard from there.

Self-check: open `index.html#test`.

## How every game teaches

All subjects run on the same small method, so a parent can learn it once and use it away from the screen.

1. **A few things at a time.** Each subject is an ordered list. Two or three items are in play; the next one joins only when one is known.
2. **Show, then ask, then help less.** A brand-new item is asked with its answer glowing: that is the lesson. Next time the glow waits four seconds, then nine, then fifteen.
3. **A wrong answer teaches too.** The app names what was picked, puts it aside and asks again.
4. **Name it again once found.** The right picture stands out, the others fade, and the word, the count or the rule is said again.
5. **What is less known comes back more often.** Never the same question twice in a row.

## Why it works this way

Each choice below names the finding behind it. Ages on the home screen are rough guides drawn from these studies, not norms.

### A screen can be active or passive

- Guidance for under-fives is shared use and little of it: the American Academy of Pediatrics discourages solo media use under two and asks parents of 2 to 5 year olds to co-view and keep it to about an hour a day; the WHO says no more than an hour of sedentary screen time at two, and less is better.
  → Everything here is built to be played by two people, and a sitting ends by itself.
- Apps help when the child is mentally active, not distracted, when the content means something and when someone shares it; sounds and animations beside the point pull attention away (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015).
  → No confetti, no score, no background music.
- The youngest toddlers in a touchscreen study learned a word only when they had to touch the object itself; touching anywhere did not help (Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek, 2016).
  → Most games ask a question with one right answer. Press, where any key works, is a starting point and not expected to teach by itself.
- Structured maths apps have raised preschoolers' maths in randomised trials: 389 four and five-year-olds in the UK over twelve weeks (Outhwaite et al., 2019); a number-sense tablet curriculum in US preschools (Schacter et al., 2016; Schacter & Jo, 2017).
  → It is reasonable to expect a well-built app to teach early maths. Those trials tested other apps, not this one.
- Screen time ends more calmly at a natural stopping point, and when the technology stops by itself; a two-minute warning made things worse (Hiniker et al., 2016).
  → The goodbye comes between two questions, with no countdown.
- Prompts built into an e-book made parents talk and ask more during shared reading (Troseth et al., 2020).
  → A line for the adult on screen, and the "How to help, and why" page.

### Words

- Two-year-olds pick the right picture for a new word easily but have mostly forgotten it five minutes later. What helps is repetition, naming the thing again once it is chosen, and few competing objects (Horst & Samuelson, 2008; Horst, Scott & Pollard, 2010; Axelsson, Churchley & Horst, 2012).
  → Three words in play, two pictures while a word is new, the word said again once found.
- A two-picture "where is the car?" touch task works from 16 months and is a validated measure of comprehension (Friend & Keplinger, 2003, 2008).
  → The format of every Learn question.
- Toddlers learned new words only when the speech was about 10 dB louder than background sound (McMillan & Saffran, 2016).
  → Chimes end before a word starts. Nothing plays under a name.
- At 21 months children learned words only from infant-directed speech; by 27 months adult-directed speech worked too (Ma et al., 2011).
  → The computer voice is slowed and raised a little, and a parent can record every word.
- Children carry what they learn from a picture to the real thing more easily when the picture is a realistic photograph (Ganea, Pickard & DeLoache, 2008).
  → Photos and real recordings.
- Two-year-olds learn colour words better when the colour comes after the noun, "the ball is red" (Ramscar et al., 2010).
  → Colours come late and are always said after the thing.

### Numbers

- Children learn what "one" means, then "two", then "three", one at a time and over many months, even when they can already recite to ten. Most two-year-olds know only "one"; two-knowers in one study averaged a little over three. Only after "three" or "four" do children grasp that the last word of a count says how many (Wynn, 1990, 1992; Sarnecka & Carey, 2008). The age at which this happens varies widely (Gunderson & Levine, 2011).
  → Numbers starts with one against two, adds three only when two is known, and so on. The age setting gives older children a head start.
- Saying a set's total and then counting it was the only training that improved three-and-a-half-year-olds' grasp of this in one study (Mix et al., 2012). A later study found counting first, with emphasis on the last word, then saying the total worked better.
  → The app does both: "Three ducks. One, two, three. Three ducks!"
- "Give me three" is the standard task for finding out which numbers a child really understands (Wynn, 1990).
  → Count. Until the child knows "three", it counts along and stops for them; after that they must stop themselves.
- Talk about numbers of things the child can see, especially sets of four and more, predicts later number knowledge better than other number talk (Gunderson & Levine, 2011).
  → Every number question shows the things. The guide asks parents to count real objects.
- Four short sessions of a board game with the numbers 1 to 10 in a line, moving one or two squares and saying each number, improved preschoolers' counting, numeral reading, comparison and number-line estimates. The same game with colours did not, and neither did a circular board (Siegler & Ramani, 2008, 2009; Ramani & Siegler, 2011).
  → Race: the same game, with a key press for each hop.

### Shapes and patterns

- Four and five-year-olds taught with odd examples as well as textbook ones, and guided to find what defines each shape, learned better than those taught directly or left to play freely (Fisher et al., 2013).
  → Once the plain triangle is found, thin, tilted and upside-down ones appear. The rule is said after each right answer: "three sides, three corners".
- Knowledge of repeating patterns at the end of preschool predicts maths achievement years later (Fyfe, Rittle-Johnson & Farran, 2019).
  → "What comes next?" is the last step of Shapes.

### A second language

- Preschoolers accept two names for one thing when the names clearly come from different languages (Au & Glusman, 1990).
  → A new word is first announced: "In French, the cow is: la vache."
- Nine-month-olds exposed to a foreign language by a live speaker learned its sounds; the same speakers on video or audio had no effect (Kuhl, Tsao & Liu, 2003).
- A review of studies from 2015 to 2025 with three to six-year-olds learning another language found interactive media helped vocabulary when paired with adult support and bilingual content (Gfeller et al.).
  → The game gives exposure and a few first words. It asks the adult to say the word too, and says plainly that a person teaches a language better than a screen. It needs a voice for the second language on the device; if there is none, it says so.

### Keyboard and mouse

- Around two and a half, children mostly fail to use a model of a space as a map of the real space; around three they succeed (DeLoache, 1987, 2000).
  → The on-screen keyboard is such a map. Hence six big zones for the youngest, stickers for the real keys, and Find labelled "around 3".
- There is little research on mouse use before three. Three-year-olds managed a simple cursor-placement task with a mouse, a joystick or a trackball (Strommen et al., 1996); four and five-year-olds point much less accurately than older children and adults (Hourcade et al., 2004).
  → The mouse track is labelled from about three, starts with moving only, and its levels follow the child up and down.
- Dragging: one study found five to six-year-olds slower and less accurate at dragging than at pointing (Joiner et al., 1998); another found drag-and-drop faster and less error-prone than click-move-click at five to seven (Donker & Reitsma, 2007).
  → Drag accepts both.
- Lift-the-flap features in a first-words book hindered word learning in two-year-olds (Shinskey, 2021).
  → Click, where animals hide under keys, is practice for the hand and not counted as word learning.

### What is not backed by a study

The exact steps in each subject, the timing of the fading hint, the "known after two unaided finds" rule, the head start given by the age setting, the six and twelve key zones and the mouse level rules are design decisions in the spirit of the findings above. Nobody has tested Kidoboard itself with children. Sets stop at five and written numbers at five (ten in Race); letters and reading are not covered.
